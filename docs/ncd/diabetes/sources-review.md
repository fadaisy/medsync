## 1. D-DM-11: C-peptide (ADA 2026 Fig. 2.1, read as an image; Holt 2021 Fig. 1 legend)

**What the figure shows** (flowchart for suspected type 1 diabetes in newly diagnosed adults, "based on data from White European populations"):

- Islet autoantibody positive → type 1 diabetes.
- Antibody negative, **under 35**: features of monogenic diabetes? Yes → test C-peptide: **<200 pmol/L → type 1**; >200 → genetic testing for monogenic diabetes. No → features of type 2? No → type 1; yes → unclear classification.
- Antibody negative, **35 or over** → unclear classification ("trial of noninsulin therapy may be appropriate") → **consider C-peptide after >3 years' duration**: **<200 → type 1; 200–600 → indeterminate, consider repeat at >5 years; >600 → type 2**.

**Legend (Holt 2021, verbatim):** "A C-peptide test is only indicated in people receiving insulin treatment. A random sample (with concurrent glucose) within 5 h of eating can replace a formal C-peptide stimulation test in the context of classification. If the result is ≥600 pmol/l, the circumstances of testing do not matter. If the result is <600 pmol/l and the concurrent glucose is <4 mmol/l (<72 mg/dl) or the person may have been fasting, consider repeating the test. Results showing very low levels (<80 pmol/l) do not need to be repeated. … Do not test C-peptide within 2 weeks of a hyperglycaemic emergency." And: "C-peptide values 200–600 pmol/l are usually consistent with type 1 diabetes or maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) but may occur in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, particularly in people with normal or low BMI or after long duration."

**Proposal.** Sign REF-DM-CLS-CPEP-LOW 200 and -HIGH 600 as read. Restore the two criteria as **supporting evidence only** (never a classification on their own), each requiring: on insulin; islet antibodies negative or not available; more than 3 years since diagnosis; no hyperglycaemic emergency in the last 2 weeks. For the high criterion, a concurrent glucose below 4 or a fasting sample makes it "repeat", not evidence. Show the figure's own caveat with it: the data are from White European adults, and ketosis-prone diabetes, common in Africa, can show low C-peptide soon after ketoacidosis. Needs two new inputs (years since diagnosis; hyperglycaemic emergency in the last 2 weeks).

## 2. D-DM-22: which sulfonylurea at which level (EMHSLU 2023)

**Levels of use** (p. 23): "Each item has a designation representing the lowest level of use. The item can be prescribed and dispensed at this level and all the levels above." NR national referral, RR regional referral, H hospital, HC4 (medical officer), HC3 (clinical officer), HC2.

**§18.5 Insulins and medicines used for diabetes** (p. 43, verbatim rows): Insulin isophane HC4 · Soluble insulin HC4 · Insulin isophane aspart HC4 · **Glimepiride Tablet 2 mg H** · **Metformin Tablet 500 mg HC3** · Pioglitazone RR · Insulin aspart 30%+70% H · Insulin glargine H · **Gliclazide Tablet 80 mg RR** · Vildagliptin RR · Dapagliflozin RR.

**Deleted from the 2016 list** (p. 24): "16. Glibenclamide Tablet 5 mg". **Added in 2023** (p. 25) include dapagliflozin, gliclazide, insulin aspart, insulin glargine, biphasic insulin aspart and glucagon. Not on the list: sitagliptin, empagliflozin, semaglutide.

**What this means.** Below general-hospital level the list has **no sulfonylurea**: a health centre IV has metformin and human insulin; a health centre III has metformin only. UCG's second line (glimepiride) is stocked from general hospitals upward.

**Proposal.** (a) DM-ACT-RX-02 adds: "EMHSLU 2023 stocks glimepiride from general hospitals upward; at a health centre, refer for it." (b) The formulary's essential-list flags follow the list: on it — metformin, glimepiride, gliclazide, soluble and isophane insulin, insulin glargine, insulin aspart, biphasic insulin aspart, pioglitazone, vildagliptin, dapagliflozin; not on it — glibenclamide (deleted), sitagliptin, empagliflozin, semaglutide. (c) Glucagon is on the 2023 list; no change here.

## 3. D-DM-26: insulin storage and clay pots

**EADSG 2019 (verbatim):** "(5) Insulin in use should be kept at room temperature and should never be kept immersed under water. Immersing insulin under water after the vial has been pierced carries a high risk of contamination, leading to loss of potency and likelihood of causing injection abscesses." "Vials in use may be kept at room temperature (20–25 °C), protected from sunlight, for 6 weeks, and 4 weeks if the temperature goes up to 30 °C, in a clean plastic box." "Keeping insulin in clay pots is likely to cause contamination as it is difficult to keep it clean: this should be avoided. Clay pots have been previously recommended [Ogle 2016]." "(3) If insulin is to be stored at home for over 2 months, it should be stored at the recommended temperature of 2–8 °C."

**Ogle 2016 (abstract):** all 13 devices (10 clay pots, a goat skin, a gourd, a wet-sand bucket) cooled insulin by 2.7–8.3 °C; "Further studies are needed on insulin stability to determine when these devices are necessary."

**What this means.** The two do not conflict: Ogle measured cooling, not insulin stability or hygiene; EADSG, the regional guideline, read Ogle and advises against clay pots for contamination, with an alternative that needs no cooling.

**Proposal.** DM-ACT-EDU-05 carries EADSG in full: unopened insulin in a fridge (2–8 °C) if kept more than 2 months, never frozen; the vial in use at room temperature in a clean plastic box, out of the sun, for up to 6 weeks (4 weeks if the room reaches 30 °C); never kept in water; clay pots avoided because they are hard to keep clean.

## 5. D-DM-42: vomiting on insulin; ketoacidosis in pregnancy

**ISPAD 2022 (children and adolescents, verbatim):** "Consider nausea and/or vomiting as a sign of insulin deficiency and ketosis until proven otherwise." (p. 913, grade E; repeated p. 919.) "If vomiting persists beyond 2 h, especially in children under 5 years old, or if hypoglycemia cannot be corrected, refer for intravenous fluids …" (p. 919).

**JBDS 2023, DKA in pregnancy (verbatim, p. 23):** "DKA in pregnancy is an obstetric emergency as it carries a risk of mortality and morbidity for the pregnant woman and the fetus. Women who are suspected of having DKA must be admitted to the delivery suite or the high dependency unit …" "DKA in pregnancy can occur with only very modest elevation of glucose levels, i.e. <13.9mmol/l, ('euglycaemic DKA') …" "Ketoacidosis should always be considered when a pregnant woman with diabetes feels unwell. These women must be assessed by a medical or diabetes team." Fetal mortality in the UK series: 16%.

**What this means.** For **children and adolescents** on insulin, vomiting is insulin deficiency until proven otherwise, whatever the strip. For **adults**, neither source makes vomiting with a negative urine strip an emergency on its own. For **pregnancy**, the glucose threshold must not gate ketoacidosis.

**Proposal.** (a) Under 16 on insulin, vomiting with a raised glucose: interrupt whatever the urine strip. (b) Adults: keep same-day, worded "measure blood ketones now if you can; otherwise repeat the urine strip in 2 hours" (no source for more). (c) Pregnant with diabetes and unwell (vomiting, abdominal pain or feeling unwell): same-day assessment by a medical or diabetes team and ketones now, whatever the glucose; raised ketones in pregnancy interrupt as an obstetric emergency. I will check each against the escalation-floor grid before it ships.
