## Entry-point structure in real healthcare booking products: what the patient is asked first, whether urgency is a separate door or a branch, and where triage sits relative to slot selection — read against NHS public-service design guidance, self-triage accuracy research, and East African/Ugandan primary evidence.

- CLAIM: The NHS — the closest public-sector analogue to a referral hospital serving mixed literacy and mixed urgency — runs TWO doors at the national level, and does so deliberately. Booking (NHS App → GP appointments) and urgency (111 online → clinical assessment) are separate products, separately branded, with separate front pages. 111 online is regulated as a Class 1 medical device; the booking flow is not.
  EVIDENCE: Research/government service design (primary, first-party). NHS.uk: 111 online is where you "answer questions about your symptoms… and you will be told what to do next", with explicit carve-outs to call 111 (under-5s, complex conditions) or 999 (life-threatening). NHS England Digital classifies NHS 111 online as a Class 1 medical device — a regulatory status the appointment-booking journey does not carry. The urgency door is therefore not a filter inside booking; it is a different, clinically-governed service.
  URL: https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/urgent-and-emergency-care-services/when-to-use-111/ and https://digital.nhs.uk/services/nhs-111-online/nhs-111-online-is-a-class-1-medical-device
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, strongly. The NHS design context (mixed literacy, mixed urgency, free-at-point-of-use, walk-in-capable population, capacity-constrained) is the least WEIRD-biased source available and the structural reason for the split — different clinical governance for a triage decision vs a scheduling decision — applies identically at Mulago. The regulatory framing (medical device) is a UK artefact, but MedSync's own 'decision support never autonomous' rule creates the same governance boundary.

- CLAIM: Inside the NHS App booking flow, WHEN is asked LAST, not first. The sequence is start page → appointment type → location → healthcare professional → check answers → confirmation, with available times shown only after those selections. No step asks the patient to declare urgency.
  EVIDENCE: First-party design record (NHS App design history — a published design history, not marketing). Quote: users "make their selections for appointment type, location and healthcare professional before being shown a list of available appointments". The redesign was validated with 85 initial participants, then 438 users across 15 research rounds and visits to 10 GP surgeries. Key research finding quoted: "users are sensitive to any friction and will easily give up and call their GP surgery."
  URL: https://design-history.nhsapp.service.nhs.uk/gp-services/2025/10/appointment-booking-updates/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The ordering principle (what/who before when) transfers; the friction finding transfers with force — the Ugandan fallback is not 'call the surgery' but 'just show up', which is cheaper and more culturally default than the UK fallback, so friction tolerance is LOWER, not higher. The specific six-step flow does not transfer: six server round-trips on intermittent bandwidth is a different cost than in the UK.

- CLAIM: NHS England explicitly refuses to collapse access to a single digital front door, and names vulnerable patients as the reason. This is a direct policy statement against the 'one entry, route behind the scenes' model.
  EVIDENCE: Government policy guidance (primary). Quotes: "Patients will always need traditional access routes such as telephone or face-to-face"; "Vulnerable patients may be disadvantaged by remote digital triage"; warns of "increasing barriers to access for certain patient groups if not supported adequately by traditional modes of access, e.g. patients with poor access to technology or learning difficulties."
  URL: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/digitally-enabled-triage/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes. Shared devices, mixed literacy and intermittent data make Uganda a harder case than the one NHSE is describing, so the warning is a fortiori. Caveat: NHSE is talking about channel (digital vs phone), not about door count within the digital channel — I am extending it by inference, and flag that.

- CLAIM: Funnelling everyone through one triage entry measurably widens inequality of access. The evidence review finds lower uptake among the most deprived and recommends keeping flexible, multiple routes rather than one clever router.
  EVIDENCE: Evidence review / research synthesis (Health Equity Evidence Centre). Online consultation use was "16% of patients in quintile 1, compared to… 22% in quintiles 3 and 4"; smartphone ownership 79% of all adults vs 57% of over-75s; minority ethnic communities showed "reduced uptake of phone or digital registration systems due to data confidentiality concerns". Recommended mitigations, graded: co-design with disadvantaged communities (low certainty), case-by-case flexibility in triage protocols (moderate certainty), pause AI triage until equity is demonstrated (very low certainty).
  URL: https://www.heec.co.uk/resource/what-works-mitigating-inequalities-in-telephone-and-digital-triage/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The deprivation gradient in digital uptake almost certainly transfers and is likely steeper; the specific UK percentages do not. The 'pause AI triage until equity shown' recommendation aligns with MedSync's existing decision-support-never-autonomous rule.

- CLAIM: Patients cannot reliably grade their own urgency. If the single entry asks 'when do you want to be seen?', the answer is clinically uninformative — and both under- and over-triage occur, not just safe over-triage.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research (two independent studies). PLOS One ED study comparing two self-triage tools against nurse-driven CTAS triage: "both the AGST and VAS tools had poor levels of agreement for each of the four presenting complaints"; notably "patients both over- and under-triaged themselves" (contradicting earlier literature that assumed over-triage dominates); authors concluded self-triage "does not appear to be a viable option for use in the ED" as an all-comers approach. The Belgian ODISSEE self-triage platform did better because it asked clinical questions rather than asking the patient to rate urgency: 85.6% accuracy on level of care but only 68.4% on full urgency category; under-triage 1.9%, over-triage 12.5%; accuracy varied by age (78.5% for 26–50 vs 61.0% for 18–25) and was not significantly improved by university education.
  URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0297321 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9508742/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes for the core finding (self-rated urgency is unreliable); the absolute accuracy numbers do not transfer — both studies are European, self-selected, high-literacy, individually-owned-device populations. The ODISSEE age gradient is a warning that a single self-declared 'when' will be least reliable in exactly the groups MedSync most needs to serve. Note this cuts BOTH designs: MedSync's existing two doors are also patient-declared. The implication is that neither door count substitutes for a red-flag safety net.

- CLAIM: No leading product asks WHEN first. They ask WHAT (reason/symptom), WHO (clinician/specialty), or WHICH SERVICE. Where urgency is expressed, it is modelled as a service category or computed from symptoms — it is an OUTPUT of assessment, never a raw input.
  EVIDENCE: Live product flows + vendor documentation. Zocdoc: "You can search for Urgent Care by symptom or visit reason, then enter your desired appointment location and choose your insurance plan" — urgency is a specialty page (/urgent-care), and time is a filter ("special hours filter at the top of their search page"), not a first question. Doctolib: the patient picks the motif de consultation from a dropdown before seeing the timetable. Ada Health computes eight time-based dispositions — self-care, self-care with pharmacy, primary care within 2–3 weeks, 2–3 days, same day, within 4 hours, urgent/emergency, call an ambulance — from a dynamic symptom assessment; the patient never states the timeframe.
  URL: https://www.zocdoc.com/urgent-care and https://doctolibpatient.zendesk.com/hc/fr/articles/360025315534 and https://ada.com/improving-care-navigation-at-sutter-with-ai/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Mostly. The principle (derive the when, don't ask it) transfers cleanly and is safety-relevant. The specific mechanisms do not: Zocdoc's insurance step is meaningless in a public referral hospital, and Ada's long adaptive questionnaire is a many-round-trip, high-literacy flow that would fail on intermittent bandwidth and shared devices.

- CLAIM: Doctolib — the largest European booking platform — deliberately does NOT offer an urgent door, and routes urgency off-platform entirely. It is a scheduling product that refuses to be a triage product.
  EVIDENCE: Vendor/product documentation plus independent comparison. Doctolib is characterised as "the reference for scheduled appointments, with no immediate access"; its own video-consultation material states that video consultation is not an emergency service and that in an emergency you should call 15 or 112.
  URL: https://www.doctolib.fr/sante/consultation-video/ and https://info.medadom.com/blog/comparatif-plateformes-teleconsultation
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: No, not as a model — MedSync cannot push its urgent patients off-platform, because at Mulago the walk-in IS the service. But it transfers as a warning: a slot-picking engine that silently absorbs urgent patients is the failure mode Doctolib designed around. Note the comparison source is a competitor's (Medadom) blog — treat the characterisation as interested, though it is consistent with Doctolib's own emergency disclaimer.

- CLAIM: Products that DO serve both urgency and scheduling show two clearly-labelled doors at the top and converge on one mechanism underneath — which is exactly ADR 0034's 'status, not entry type', validated in the wild.
  EVIDENCE: Live product flows + vendor help documentation. Practo's homepage leads with two distinct primary buttons: "Instant Video Consultation – Connect within 60 secs" and "Find Doctors Near You – Confirmed appointments", plus a separate "Book an appointment for an in-clinic consultation" section. Teladoc's help centre shows the two request types converging on one waiting room: on-demand users "enter the waiting room as soon as you have requested your video visit", scheduled users "enter the waiting room at the day and time you have requested".
  URL: https://www.practo.com/ and https://www.teladochealth.com/helpcenter/article/about-visits-and-troubleshooting-faqs
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly — and this is the most transferable competitive evidence, because Practo is an Indian mass-market product serving a walk-in-normal, price-sensitive, mixed-literacy population rather than a US/EU private one. The video-consult modality does not transfer to a physical OPD, but the top-level two-button structure over a shared backend does.

- CLAIM: In East Africa the dominant, actually-adopted patient entry point is a REQUEST (call-back, USSD, WhatsApp), not a calendar. Nobody in this market leads with a date picker.
  EVIDENCE: Vendor descriptions of live products. Rocket Health (Uganda's leading digital health service): access is via USSD *280# or a toll-free line — "When you dial *280# and choose the doctor consultation option, you will be able to make payments and request a call-back", 24/7, in English and several local languages, via chat, voice and video. MYDAWA (Kenya): "You can easily schedule through the MYDAWA platform, and a licensed medical expert will call you back", 8am–10pm. Zuri Health: services accessible via WhatsApp bot and SMS.
  URL: https://rockethealth.africa/faqs/ and https://mydawa.com/telehealth and https://www.zuri.health/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — this IS the Ugandan/East African evidence, though it is vendor self-description rather than research, and these are private pay-per-use consumer services, not a public referral hospital. The structural point stands: the 'get in line and we will reach you' pattern is the region's proven entry primitive, while slot-picking is the unproven import.

- CLAIM: Ugandan primary evidence: urgent presentations are roughly one in five OPD attenders — not an edge case — and adding triage to the front of the queue improved flow for the non-urgent while urgent patients trended WORSE. A same-day door is load-bearing, and a poorly-placed triage step can actively harm the urgent stream.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research (prospective quality-improvement study, Dr Ambrosoli Memorial Hospital, Kalongo, Uganda; Int J Emerg Med, Oct 2025). Emergency/urgent cases were 16.4% of 304 patients pre-implementation and 22.8% of 246 post. After introducing the Interagency Integrated Triage Tool, time to provider fell 23 minutes overall (95% CI −35.49 to −12.03, p<0.001), but this was driven by non-urgent patients (−31.44 min); urgent/emergency patients showed "a trend towards increased time to provider" (+5.62 min, not significant). Separately at Mulago's own outpatient clinics, "satisfaction was lower among clients reporting longer waiting time (>2 h)".
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12495705/ and https://academic.oup.com/intqhc/article/23/5/516/1864829
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — this is Ugandan data. Caveat: Kalongo is a rural general hospital, not a national referral centre, so the urgent fraction at Mulago's IM OPD may differ; and the Mulago satisfaction study is older. The directional warning — that a triage layer can slow the urgent stream while speeding the routine one — is the finding that most directly threatens a 'route it behind the scenes' design.

- CLAIM: Ugandan willingness-to-use evidence for appointment apps exists but is badly non-representative, and should NOT be cited as proof that Mulago patients will adopt slot-booking.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research with a sampling problem I am flagging explicitly. MobiCare study, Mbarara Municipality, five PRIVATE general hospitals, 2019: 100.0% (97/97) of patients willing to use, 96.9% (31/32) of providers. But the same sample reported 100% smartphone ownership, 100% internet use, 99% prior app experience, and 64% had already scheduled appointments before. Median waiting time 120 minutes (range 0–720); 75% dissatisfied with waiting times.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13034015/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: No — and that is the finding. An urban private-hospital sample with 100% smartphone penetration is not the Mulago public OPD population. The waiting-time dissatisfaction (75%, median 120 min) transfers; the adoption figures do not. Anyone citing this study as evidence that Ugandan patients will happily book slots is over-reading it.

BEARING ON ONE-DOOR-VS-TWO: RECOMMENDATION: KEEP TWO DOORS. The evidence does not support collapsing to a single "when do you want to be seen?" entry, and on one point it argues actively against it.

Four independent lines converge:

(1) NO PRECEDENT. I searched specifically for a healthcare product whose first patient question is "when do you want to be seen (today / soon / a date)" and found none. Every product I could reach asks WHAT (Zocdoc: symptom or visit reason; Doctolib: motif de consultation), WHO (NHS App: appointment type, location, professional), or WHICH SERVICE (Practo, Teladoc) first, and treats time as a filter or an output. Ada goes furthest in the opposite direction: it emits eight urgency timeframes as the RESULT of a clinical assessment, never as a question. The proposed design would be novel, on a clinical surface, in the hardest possible deployment context. That is not fatal, but it removes the "everyone does this" justification.

(2) THE STRUCTURE THE USER WANTS TO REMOVE IS THE ONE THE MARKET CONVERGED ON. Practo — the most contextually comparable product, serving a walk-in-normal, mixed-literacy, price-sensitive mass market — leads with exactly two buttons: "Instant Video Consultation — Connect within 60 secs" and "Find Doctors Near You — Confirmed appointments". Teladoc shows the two request types resolving into one waiting room. That is precisely ADR 0034's thesis (one mechanism, two declared statuses) already validated in production. The NHS goes further and splits urgency into a separately-governed service. ADR 0034 is in good company; the two doors are the visible surface of a correct backend model, not a redundancy.

(3) THE SINGLE "WHEN" QUESTION IS CLINICALLY EMPTY AND SLIGHTLY DANGEROUS. Self-rated urgency agrees poorly with clinician triage and errs in both directions (PLOS One), and self-triage accuracy degrades in younger and less-advantaged users (ODISSEE). A "today / soon / a date" question dressed as a scheduling convenience invites the patient to make a clinical judgement in a UI that gives them no clinical information to make it with — and then routes them on that judgement. The two-door design is more honest: "Join today's queue" is a declaration of intent to travel and wait, not a claim about acuity. Note this cuts both ways and is my strongest caveat: MedSync's existing doors are ALSO patient-declared, so neither design gives you triage. The real safety requirement — a red-flag interrupt that can escalate a patient out of either door — is independent of door count and should not be traded against it.

(4) THE TWO DOORS DESCRIBE TWO DIFFERENT REAL-WORLD ACTS, NOT TWO TIMEFRAMES. "Join today's queue" commits the patient to physically travelling to Mulago now and waiting (median OPD waits of ~2 hours are documented locally, and ~1 in 5 OPD attenders is urgent). "Book a future visit" is a promise about a date. Collapsing them makes "today" read as just another slot on a calendar — which hides the travel-and-wait commitment behind a scheduling metaphor. Rocket Health, MYDAWA and Zuri Health all built their Ugandan/Kenyan entry points as REQUESTS (USSD call-back, WhatsApp) rather than calendars, which suggests the queue door is the primary door in this market, not a secondary branch to be tucked inside a booking flow.

WHAT I WOULD CHANGE INSTEAD OF COLLAPSING (the defensible version of the user's instinct):

- Keep two doors, but stop making them symmetrical. The walk-in door should be visually and hierarchically primary — it is the majority path and the culturally default one. The current equal-weight presentation is the real problem, not the count.
- Relabel by ACT, not by time. "Come to the hospital today" / "Book a date to come back" beats "Join today's queue" / "Book a future visit", because it names the travel commitment.
- Add the red-flag interrupt to BOTH doors, not as a third door. This is the NHS 111 function and it is the thing actually missing; it satisfies the safety gap that the self-triage literature exposes, and it is orthogonal to door count.
- If a single entry is still wanted for funnel reasons, make the first question WHAT (reason for visit), not WHEN — consistent with every product studied — and derive the routing. But note this costs an extra round trip and an extra literacy demand, and I found no evidence it converts better.

CONFIDENCE: Moderate-to-high on "do not make WHEN the first question" (four independent sources, no counter-evidence found). Moderate on "keep exactly two doors" — the precedent is consistent but no one has run the controlled comparison. See gaps.
GAPS: NO EVIDENCE FOUND: any healthcare product whose first patient question is 'when do you want to be seen?' with today/soon/date options. I searched for this pattern directly and could not find a single live example, published case study, or design-system pattern. This is a real absence, not a search failure I am papering over — but absence of a documented example is weaker evidence than a documented failure would be.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND: any published usability study, A/B test, or conversion comparison between a single time-first entry and separate book/queue entries in healthcare. Nobody appears to have run this experiment publicly. The recommendation therefore rests on convergent precedent and safety reasoning, not on a head-to-head result.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND: an NHS/GDS design pattern or research note stating that users mis-categorise themselves at a route-choice page. I searched specifically for this (the classic 'users don't know which category they are' argument that would support collapsing) and could not source it. The NHS question-ordering guidance says the opposite-ish — 'Get your filter questions in early' — but it is about form efficiency, not clinical routing, and I will not stretch it.; Could not reach several live flows directly: Zocdoc's homepage, One Medical, and the Babylon booking-redesign UX case study all returned HTTP 403; Livi's marketing site exposes only a single 'See a clinician' call to action without the flow behind it. Zocdoc and Doctolib findings therefore rest on their own help/landing pages and secondary descriptions rather than my walking the live funnel.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND: any Ugandan or East African public-hospital patient-facing app that offers slot booking at scale, with published adoption data. The Mulago online-scheduling work I found is a student thesis, not an evaluated deployment; the one evaluated Ugandan appointment-app study (MobiCare) sampled private urban hospitals with 100% smartphone ownership. There is effectively no evidence base for how a Mulago OPD population behaves at a digital entry point — the decision has to be made without it, and should be instrumented once live.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND on bandwidth or round-trip cost of booking flows in low-connectivity settings. I could not find measured data on drop-off per step on intermittent connections, so my inference that a six-step NHS-style flow is too chatty for Mulago is inference, labelled as such, not measured fact.; Could not verify Helium Health's / HeliumDoc's actual patient entry flow — the page returned empty content and no independent description of the first screen exists in accessible sources. Nigeria's largest health-tech patient product is therefore missing from the comparison set.; The Johannesburg 'booked appointments in a large HIV clinic' acceptability study (medRxiv 2022.06.30.22277110) was 403-blocked. It is the closest African evidence on whether a booked-appointment model displaces walk-in behaviour, and I could not read it.; Where triage sits relative to registration in a Ugandan OPD is under-documented: the Kalongo study reports outcomes but, on the text I could retrieve, does not state whether triage occurred before or after registration/queueing. That sequencing is exactly what MedSync needs to mirror, and I could not confirm it.

## Same-day care, walk-in queues and virtual queueing — the mechanism MedSync's second door serves

- CLAIM: Every live product that runs a genuine same-day queue goes out of its way to tell the user it is NOT an appointment. The disclaimer is not fine print — it is the core of the product's promise.
  EVIDENCE: Live product surface (provider/vendor). UC San Diego Health's urgent care FAQ, fetched directly, reads verbatim: "Save My Spot is not an appointment. You choose a time so we can plan for your arrival, but it is your place in line, not a guaranteed visit time." And: "We treat patients based on medical need, so those with more serious conditions may be seen first." The same construction recurs across US urgent care (MedStar: "Your arrival time is not an appointment... Your place in line may change if another patient has a more serious condition"; Centra Health: "it is important to remember that this is not an appointment"; UMMS: "Choosing a check-in time is not an appointment"). Note: I could fetch UCSD directly; MedStar/Centra/UMMS/CareNow/Banner returned 403 or geoblocks, so those are search-index snippets attributed to their own pages, not pages I read.
  URL: https://health.ucsd.edu/care/walk-in-same-day/urgent-care/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes. This is a language/contract finding, not a market finding. If anything it transfers harder: Mulago's queue is more re-orderable by clinical need than a US urgent care's, so any UI that implies a time promise is a bigger integrity problem. It is also the closest thing in this evidence base to MedSync's 'no fabricated data' rule applied to a queue.

- CLAIM: A high-volume public service with mixed literacy and mixed device access — the California DMV — deliberately runs TWO named doors side by side, labelled by mechanism, and has kept them that way.
  EVIDENCE: Live product flow (fetched directly via curl, Sept 2026). The dmv.ca.gov Appointments navigation contains three sibling items: "Locations & Hours", "Get in Line Now", "Schedule Appointment". They are not nested under a 'when do you want to come' question; they are two separate entry points. Secondary reporting describes Get in Line Now as giving "a number that tells you your place in line" with notification as your turn approaches.
  URL: https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/appointments/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The user population (walk-in-by-default, long physical queues, mixed digital literacy, a captive monopoly provider people cannot shop around) is a much better analogue for Mulago than any private US clinic. What does not transfer: the DMV's capacity is predictable and its 'appointments' are weeks out, and its users are not sick.

- CLAIM: The one-door collapse does exist commercially — but only where the provider can actually schedule its same-day capacity into slots. It is a capacity fact, not a UX preference.
  EVIDENCE: Vendor self-description. CVS MinuteClinic has collapsed to a single object: it "isn't a traditional walk-in clinic" and does not offer on-demand in-person visits; a walk-in becomes an appointment booked at the in-store kiosk, "based on availability", and does "not guarantee you will be seen". Solv markets same-day urgent care entirely as bookable time slots — "book a same-day appointment in under two minutes" — and describes "AI-powered queuing" that "blends book-ahead and walk-in patients... by automatically shifting the slots you show online". Both pages returned 403 to direct fetch; content is from the search index attributed to those URLs.
  URL: https://www.solvhealth.com/for-providers/scheduling
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: No, not in its current form. Both examples presuppose a clinic that can convert unscheduled demand into slots because volume is modest, predictable and elastic (staff can be added, visits are short, patients can be turned away). Mulago's OPD cannot: demand exceeds schedulable capacity, so 'today' cannot honestly be rendered as a time slot. This is the strongest single reason the US one-door pattern does not port.

- CLAIM: OpenTable is the closest live precedent for exactly what the user is proposing — a when-first single entry that routes to a queue behind the scenes — and it is instructive that even there, the two OBJECTS stay distinct at the moment of commitment.
  EVIDENCE: Vendor documentation. The diner enters through the normal reservation widget (date, time, party size). The "Join Waitlist" button surfaces in-flow only when no reservation is available within the next 90 minutes. Once on the waitlist, "guests can view their place in line and the party sizes ahead of them" — position, not a quoted time — and can join before arriving. Restaurants can enable/disable the online waitlist per shift. Caveat: OpenTable's support site is a JS app and returned a CSS error to direct fetch; this is the search index's rendering of that support article plus OpenTable's product page.
  URL: https://support.opentable.com/s/article/Offer-your-waitlist-online?language=en_US
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The routing logic transfers cleanly — ask when, offer a slot if one exists, otherwise offer a position — and it is a strong existence proof that one entry point can serve two mechanisms without lying. What does not transfer: the 90-minute threshold, and the fact that a restaurant can refuse you. It also shows the collapse is of the ENTRY, not of the object: the diner still ends up holding either a reservation or a place in line, named differently.

- CLAIM: Showing a position number is not neutrally good — the psychology literature says it helps while people are behind you and actively hurts when you are last.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research, two papers. Zhou & Soman, Journal of Consumer Research 29(4):517–530 (2003): as the number of people behind a consumer increases, they are in a more positive affective state and "the likelihood of reneging is lower", explained by downward social comparison. Buell, Management Science 67(3):1430–1452 (2021), one grocery-store field observation plus four online virtual-queue experiments: "waiting in last place diminishes wait satisfaction while increasing the probabilities of switching and abandoning queues"; customers who are last in line are roughly twice as likely to switch or renege, partially because with nobody behind them they cannot make the downward comparison that reassures them waiting is worthwhile.
  URL: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3619
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly, with a real caveat. The mechanism (social comparison) is plausibly universal and both studies are lab/field consumer settings, not clinics, and not African. But the design implication is concrete and cheap to honour: show "N ahead of you", never "you are last", and consider showing people behind. Note the abandonment risk here is a clinical risk at Mulago, not a lost sale — a patient who leaves the queue leaves untreated.

- CLAIM: The evidence for showing an estimated wait time is weak-to-null, and the evidence that an INACCURATE estimate does harm is strong. MedSync's position-only choice is supported.
  EVIDENCE: (a) Peer-reviewed RCT, null result: 190 patients, King Fahad Hofuf Hospital, Saudi Arabia, Nov 2023. Intervention group got an estimated waiting time at triage (computed from last month's mean wait by triage category). Satisfaction 3.51±1.12 vs 3.49±1.12 control, P=0.962 — "No statistically significant difference in the level of satisfaction between patients who were provided with the estimated waiting time and those who were not." (b) Peer-reviewed simulation, harm result: JAMIA, March 2026, discrete-event model of three Hong Kong EDs (317,519 visits). Inaccurate prediction methods showed a U-shaped curve — helpful at low adoption, damaging at high adoption. With the 95th-percentile method at 100% adoption "the system-wide average wait time increased by 96% compared to the case with no information", with LWBS and wait-time variance also worsening. The authors frame it as the first systematic evidence that poorly implemented wait-time information systems can harm ED operations.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13197183/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, and arguably more so. The harm mechanism is patients reacting to a published number by redirecting or abandoning; the Hong Kong model is a public, free-at-point-of-use, congested ED network, which is a closer structural analogue to Mulago than US private urgent care. MedSync has fewer honest signals than a Hong Kong ED does, so any estimate it produced would sit at the inaccurate end of that paper's spectrum.

- CLAIM: The counter-evidence for estimates is real but does not say what MedSync would need it to say — the one strongly positive trial does not report what it actually displayed.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed RCT with an important reporting gap. Prospective randomised single-blinded trial, paediatric ED, Tehran, Iran, July–Aug 2020, 236 patients (118/arm, ESI 4–5). Perceived waiting time 11.9±7.4 min in the intervention arm vs 32.8±8.7 min control (p<0.001); satisfaction significantly higher (p<0.001), with no parent in the intervention arm reporting below 'good'. But on close reading the paper describes the queue management system only as streamlining patient flow and does not specify whether patients saw a position, an estimated time, or both — so it cannot be cited for 'estimates beat position'.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8464010/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. Middle-income public paediatric ED, closer than US settings. But the unreported display makes it useless for the specific one-vs-two and position-vs-estimate decisions. I am flagging it because it is the paper most likely to be waved at this decision, and it does not settle it.

- CLAIM: The most-cited authority on queue psychology — Maister's 'The Psychology of Waiting Lines' — is a 1985 practitioner essay, not empirical research, and almost everything written about queue UX is downstream of it.
  EVIDENCE: Practitioner essay, widely reproduced (I could not extract text from the Columbia-hosted PDF; content is from multiple independent reproductions of the propositions). Its relevant propositions are 'Uncertain waits are longer than known, finite waits' — illustrated with exactly our case, 'the doctor will see you in thirty minutes' vs 'the doctor will see you soon' — plus 'Anxiety makes waits seem longer', 'Unexplained waits are longer than explained waits', 'Unfair waits are longer than equitable waits', and 'Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time'. Labelling this matters: Maister points AWAY from position-only, and every vendor blog that says 'always show a wait estimate' is citing him, not evidence.
  URL: https://www.columbia.edu/~ww2040/4615S13/Psychology_of_Waiting_Lines.pdf
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Unknown, and worth saying so. It is a 40-year-old essay about American service businesses with no empirical base and no cross-cultural work behind it. Its 'uncertain waits' proposition is in direct tension with the two peer-reviewed findings above; where they conflict, the RCT and the simulation should win. A defensible middle: satisfy Maister with EXPLANATION (why the order changes, that sicker patients go first, that the queue is append-only and audited) rather than with a fabricated number.

- CLAIM: At Mulago specifically, the dominant variable is arrival time, not mechanism — which is the single best argument in this evidence base FOR asking 'when'.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed/dissertation research, the actual beachhead site. Musinguzi, C. (2013), Makerere University School of Public Health, Assessment Centre, General OPD, Mulago Hospital, n=401: median 346 minutes in the assessment centre, with "5% of this time with health worker and 95% of this time waiting for care". Waiting was longest at registration and pharmacy; overall waiting time rose by 3.4, 3.1 and 4.6 minutes per additional patient in the registration, triage and pharmacy queues respectively; patients arriving after 11am had longer waits than early arrivals, and Fridays were worse than Mondays. The recommendation was a system that limits how many patients report to OPD at once.
  URL: https://makir.mak.ac.ug/handle/10570/6486
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — this IS Uganda, and it is Mulago. The caution is age (2013, pre-Kiruddu/Kawempe split) and that it is a single-centre dissertation. The design implication is specific: influencing WHEN someone arrives has measurable value at this site, but that argues for an arrival-time nudge inside the same-day door, not for merging the two mechanisms into one picker.

- CLAIM: An African digital booking system does work and does cut waits — but its failure modes are data cost, comprehension and downtime, all of which punish a many-screen entry flow.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed cross-sectional study. SkipTheQ, tertiary hospital outpatient pharmacy, Gauteng, South Africa, 297 adult users. Mobile/web pre-booking of collection slots with SMS/WhatsApp confirmation. Average wait fell from 87 minutes to 29 minutes (67% reduction). But: 89.9% owned smartphones, 82.8% had internet access, and only 76.4% could afford mobile data; satisfaction was predicted by data affordability and by whether the booking confirmed and gave the preferred slot; reported problems were system downtime (21.8%), unprepared medication (18.8%), fully booked slots (12.5%) and "inadequate patient understanding of how to use the STQ system correctly" (15.6%). The authors conclude assisted digital access and digital-literacy support are required.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13471238/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Largely yes — African public tertiary hospital, same class of user, same constraints MedSync names (data cost, mixed literacy, shared devices). The transferable warning: ~1 in 6 users did not understand the system. Whatever the entry point is, it has to be comprehensible on one screen and survive a failed round trip. That cuts against a multi-step when-picker with a date calendar, and also against two doors whose difference is not obvious in one glance.

- CLAIM: The 'wait at home, we'll text you' value proposition that justifies virtual queueing in the US is materially weaker in Uganda, because being reachable at the moment your turn comes is not guaranteed.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed cross-sectional study. Mayuge district, eastern Uganda, 2,019 women aged 25–49: 76.1% owned a cellphone, a further 14.5% of non-owners accessed a family member's phone and 2.0% a neighbour's, giving 92.7% with any access and 7.3% with none. The paper flags shared access as a distinct risk for health communication and cites Kenyan work finding 55.2% of phone-sharers vs 70.7% of owners were comfortable receiving screening results by SMS, and that non-owners were less likely to attend treatment.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11622906/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, with a caveat: Mayuge is rural/semi-urban eastern Uganda, not Kampala, so Mulago's catchment is likely better connected — I found no Kampala-specific figure. The implication holds either way: MedSync's second door mostly buys a patient a recorded position and a declared intent, not the ability to wait at home safely. Designing the queue as if it were a remote-notification product would overpromise.

- CLAIM: The strongest government-backed 'one front door' precedent — NHS total triage — collapses the doors by asking about CLINICAL NEED, not about WHEN, and the practice, not the patient, decides what happens next. It also mandates keeping non-digital doors open.
  EVIDENCE: Government service guidance. NHS England, Digitally enabled triage: "Every patient that contacts the practice is first triaged before an appointment is arranged. All patient requests are screened and signposted by the practice to the next step of their care journey." On exclusion: "patients will always need traditional access routes such as telephone or face-to-face. This is essential for preventing worsening health inequalities," and vulnerable patients "may be disadvantaged by remote digital triage". The Modern General Practice model describes a single care navigation process so requests by phone, online or in person "are directed to the right care professional first time". Note the guidance is explicitly principle-based: it cites CQC/GMC/HEE guidance and presents no outcome evaluation.
  URL: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/digitally-enabled-triage/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly, and the mismatch is the point. The organising question that makes the NHS's one door work is 'what is wrong with you', answered by a clinician — not 'when would you like to come', answered by the patient. Uganda's walk-in norm also means the in-person door is the default, not the fallback, so 'keep the non-digital route open' is even more binding here.

- CLAIM: A when-first entry is a patient self-triage question in disguise, and laypeople self-triage at roughly coin-flip-to-60% accuracy.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed systematic review. npj Digital Medicine (2025) synthesis of self-triage accuracy: symptom-assessment applications 11.5–90.0% (moderate, highly variable), large language models 57.8–76.0%, and laypeople 47.3–62.4%. Related reviews report triage accuracy for symptom checkers of 48.8–90.1%, generally inferior to or more risk-averse than clinicians, and identify under-triage as the safety-critical failure.
  URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01566-6
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly — these studies are overwhelmingly US/European and vignette-based, and none are Ugandan, so treat the exact percentages as non-transferable. The directional point does transfer and is the strongest clinical-safety argument in this angle: 'today / soon / a specific date' is not a scheduling preference, it is an urgency judgement being delegated to the patient. Whatever the entry design, MedSync must not let that judgement pass unexamined into a booking.

BEARING ON ONE-DOOR-VS-TWO: Direct bearing, and from this angle the evidence lands mostly on KEEP TWO OBJECTS, with one specific concession to the one-door idea.

What the evidence actually establishes:

1. The two things are not the same object and no serious product pretends they are. Every live same-day queue product I could read spends its copy insisting the ticket is NOT an appointment (UCSD verbatim: "it is your place in line, not a guaranteed visit time"). A slot is a promise about a time; a position is a promise about an order, re-orderable by clinical need. Collapsing the entry is arguable; collapsing the object is a fabricated promise, and at Mulago — where the queue genuinely re-orders by acuity — it is the exact failure mode MedSync's no-fabricated-data rule exists to prevent. ADR 0034's internal model ("a status, not an entry type") is right; the question is only how many buttons sit on top of it.

2. One-door collapses in healthcare happen only where same-day capacity is schedulable. MinuteClinic and Solv both dissolve the walk-in into a slot, and both can only do it because they can convert unscheduled demand into appointments and turn people away. Mulago OPD cannot. The pattern's evidence base is US private retail/urgent care and does not transfer. This is the single clearest WEIRD-bias flag in the angle.

3. There IS one live precedent for exactly the proposed flow, and it is worth copying — partially. OpenTable asks when first and surfaces "Join Waitlist" in-flow only when nothing is bookable soon. Note two things it does: the entry is unified but the resulting object is still named differently, and the waitlist shows position and parties ahead, not a time. That is a one-door entry with two honest exits. If the user wants one door, that is the shape the evidence supports — not a date picker that silently yields a queue ticket.

4. The strongest argument AGAINST the "ask WHEN" framing specifically is clinical, not aesthetic. "Today / soon / a date" is an urgency self-assessment wearing scheduling clothes, and laypeople self-triage at 47–62% accuracy. Two explicitly labelled doors at least make the patient's declaration visible and auditable as a declaration. A when-picker reframes the same decision as a convenience preference, which is precisely the framing that makes under-triage invisible. If MedSync collapses to one door, the routing question should be about need or about what the patient wants to happen ("I need to be seen today" vs "I want a time with a clinician"), not about calendar convenience.

5. The counter-argument FOR a when-first door is real and specific to this site: at Mulago, arrival time is the dominant lever (median 346 minutes, 95% of it waiting, worse after 11am, worse on Fridays). Asking "when" has genuine operational value. But that argues for an arrival-time nudge inside the same-day door — "come after 11 and you will wait longer" — not for merging the mechanisms.

6. On position-only vs estimate, MedSync's existing choice is the better-evidenced one and should be kept. A null RCT on showing estimates, a 2026 JAMIA simulation where an inaccurate estimate at high adoption raised system-wide wait by 96%, and no honest signal to build an estimate from — that settles it. The Maister proposition pulling the other way is a 1985 essay, not research, and its "uncertain waits feel longer" concern should be answered with explanation (why the order changes, that sicker patients go first, that the record is append-only) rather than with a number. One concrete amendment: Buell's last-place aversion means "you are last" roughly doubles abandonment, so show "N ahead of you", never a bare last-place position, and consider surfacing people behind.

Net recommendation from this angle: keep two doors, but rewrite them to name the promise rather than the mechanism — "Get a time with a clinician" vs "Be seen today (you'll get a place in line, not a time)". If the parent decides to collapse, collapse to the OpenTable shape (one entry, two clearly-named exits, position-only on the queue side, urgency-framed rather than calendar-framed), and never to a date picker that quietly issues queue tickets.
GAPS: No usability study, A/B test or published research found that directly compares a single entry point against two entry points for patient booking. I searched for it explicitly. The only thing close was an unpublished portfolio UX case study (n=6, remote moderated) that tested both flows separately without comparing the entry choice. This is the central question and it has no direct evidence base in either direction — the recommendation above is reasoning from adjacent evidence, not from a trial.; No evidence found on booking/queue INTERFACE design in Ugandan or East African hospitals. There is a solid literature on waiting TIMES in Ugandan, Kenyan, Tanzanian and Ethiopian OPDs, and one South African digital booking evaluation, but nothing on how patients interpret the entry-point choice, what they call these objects in Luganda or English, or whether 'queue position' is even the mental model patients hold at a Ugandan OPD. This is the biggest unknown and is answerable only by going to Mulago.; Could not verify the Kwena et al. 2015 RCT of a designated-time appointment system at a busy HIV clinic in Kenya (J Int AIDS Soc, doi 10.7448/IAS.18.1.19876) — Wiley returned 403. It is the most relevant African RCT on whether appointment-time assignment actually changes patient arrival behaviour and should be retrieved before the parent relies on any claim about appointment feasibility in this setting.; The one strongly positive queue-management RCT (Tehran paediatric ED) does not report what was actually displayed to patients — position, estimated time, or both — so it cannot adjudicate position-only vs estimate. I looked specifically for this and the paper is silent.; No study found measuring left-without-being-seen or queue abandonment as a function of position-only vs estimated-time display in a real clinic. The evidence here is a simulation (Hong Kong EDs) plus consumer/call-centre analogues (Buell, Zhou & Soman, delay-announcement literature). Both the queue-psychology work and the delay-announcement work assume numerate WEIRD consumers. This is a live risk for a position-only display and I could not resolve it.

## Booking/health UX under Ugandan constraints: low bandwidth, shared low-end devices, mixed literacy, intermittent connectivity, walk-in-dominant care culture. Specifically: does the HCI4D / Sub-Saharan-Africa evidence base overturn the Western "one smart entry that asks WHEN" pattern, or support MedSync's existing two doors?

- CLAIM: For low-literate and low-education users, navigation DEPTH is the documented killer, not the number of options on a screen. A flat multi-page list beat a 4-level hierarchy on both speed and accuracy: mean 25s vs 65.5s per task (t(18)=4.6, p<0.001) and 100% vs 80% tasks correct (t(18)=-3, p=0.0077), with 10 non-literate users per arm in Bangalore. Authors' recommendation verbatim: "keeping navigation linear to the extent possible and to minimize hierarchical depth in UIs for non-literate users." Qualitative failure mode is important: participants "did not seem to understand the concept of nesting, or that the top graphic in a hierarchy represented a group of pages," and "did not remember how to navigate back to higher levels once they had gone down the incorrect path."
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research (Medhi, Lakshmanan, Toyama, Cutrell, INTERACT 2013, LNCS 8118 pp.497-504). Controlled between-subjects study, n=20. I extracted the numbers from the authors' own PDF, not a secondary summary. Caveat: small n, India, touchscreen-novice participants, 2013, and the hierarchy tested was 4 levels deep with branching factor ~3 over 40 items — far deeper than anything in a book-or-queue flow.
  URL: https://cutrell.org/papers/Medhi-INTERACT2013-ListVsHierachyOnMobiles.pdf
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The population (low-literate, device-novice, LMIC) is the right one and Uganda is arguably closer to it than 2013 Bangalore on literacy. But the manipulation tested (4-level nesting) is not what MedSync is choosing between. The transferable lesson is 'keep the tree shallow', not 'show fewer buttons'.

- CLAIM: The same finding replicates with education, not just literacy, as the predictor, and survives removing text entirely: in a 60-participant study in low-income Indian communities, textual literacy and Raven's Progressive Matrices scores both predicted performance navigating UI hierarchies "even when the UI is text-free." The authors' design recommendation is to limit "the complexity of information organization to straight lists whenever possible, or very shallow hierarchies with easy, obvious ways of navigating up and down the trees."
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research (Medhi et al., CHI 2013, 'Some Evidence for the Impact of Limited Education on Hierarchical User Interface Navigation', pp.2813-2822), reported via Microsoft Research's own write-up because the ACM full text is paywalled (403). This is the primary authors' institution summarising their own study, so one step removed from the paper.
  URL: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/helping-the-low-literate-learn-to-navigate-through-user-interfaces/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes for the mechanism, with a caveat. The finding is that formal schooling teaches abstract categorisation, and that this — not reading ability — is what hierarchical UIs tax. Ugandan walk-in patients at a national referral hospital span the full education range, so the constraint applies. Note the explicit 'very shallow hierarchies' escape hatch: the authors do not say one level is harmful.

- CLAIM: The synthesised HCI4D guideline set derived from two decades of literature makes this a named, actionable rule — SARAL G7, verbatim: "Simplify navigation structure: Minimize menu hierarchies. Prefer linear navigation or flatten menu hierarchies to reduce navigation complexity." Adjacent rules that bear directly here: G3 minimalist screens ("Minimize the use of text and visual components in each screen"), G5 avoid jargon ("technical terminology is confusing for low-literate users, even if it is presented in the local language"), G2 leverage numerical literacy (numbers are comprehensible where words are not), and a repeated warning against scroll bars, because "horizontal and vertical scrolling is less intuitive for low-literacy users." On icons, the survey cites Medhi's finding that action icons are actively misread — a utensils icon meant to convey 'washing dishes' was read as 'the kitchen' — and that static drawings outperform photorealistic images for concepts but break down for actions.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research synthesis (Srivastava, Kapania, Tuli, Singh, PACM HCI 5(CSCW1), 2021). Literature review of ~130 HCI4D sources plus evaluation with 20 UI/UX practitioners. I extracted the guideline text from the authors' own hosted PDF. Note it is a synthesis of prior evidence, not new empirical work.
  URL: https://anupriyatuli.github.io/publications/2021_CSCW.pdf
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Mostly. The underlying corpus is heavily India/Bangladesh/Pakistan with some South Africa and Kenya work, so it is LMIC-grounded but not Uganda-specific. The icon finding is the most directly actionable: it rules out icon-only entry points, which matters because a 'calendar icon vs queue icon' pair of doors would be exactly the failure mode described. Labelled buttons, not glyphs.

- CLAIM: Appointment booking in an African public hospital demonstrably fails as a scheduling mechanism, in a way that is not a UI problem and cannot be fixed by a better booking screen. Audit of 350 patients in a Nigerian public hospital ultrasound unit: 318 (90.9%) had scheduled appointments, yet only 26 (8.18%) arrived on time, 292 (91.82%) arrived late, and 71 (18.25%) never showed. Mean wait 132.11 minutes; 78.3% waited over an hour. The system was structurally fake: 51.26% of patients were all given an 8:00am slot, blocks of 20+ patients on a third of days, and radiologists arriving between 8:17 and 9:29am. Authors' conclusion: "Ineffective appointment booking system is the primary cause of congestion."
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research (J Family Med Prim Care, 2019). Prospective descriptive audit. Numbers extracted from the PMC full text.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6618229/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, strongly. This is the single most transferable study I found: West African public tertiary hospital, exactly the 'appointments exist on paper but are block-scheduled fiction' pattern. Mulago's own documented ~5-hour assessment-centre waits and load-driven queueing are the same system. The implication is that MedSync's door (A) currently promises a fidelity the hospital cannot deliver.

- CLAIM: Non-attendance is the norm, not the exception, across Sub-Saharan African public facilities — and in Uganda specifically. South African rural regional hospital: DNA rates of 20–40% depending on clinic, with the top reasons being unaware of the date (16%), out of area (11%), confusion over the date (11%). Uganda, HIV care across a national dataset: 158,266 of clinic visits (10.7%) were missed and 74.9% of patients missed at least one visit, stable at 9.1–13.2% across 2010–2023.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research. (a) Frost et al., Afr J Prim Health Care Fam Med 2016 (South Africa, missed-appointment reasons). (b) Visit-level prediction of missed HIV appointments in Uganda, 2026 (national-scale retrospective cohort). Both primary studies; I took the figures from search-returned abstracts/full text rather than reading both PDFs end to end.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5387369/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes. The Uganda HIV figure is Ugandan primary data. Caveat on read-across: HIV care is the best-organised, most-funded, most-appointment-native service in the country and it still loses ~10% of visits; general internal-medicine OPD at Mulago will be worse, not better. 'Confusion over the date' being a top-three named cause is a direct UI-relevant signal.

- CLAIM: Patients in these settings rationally defect from appointment times toward queue position, and arrive before dawn to secure it. Documented behaviour: patients "queue around four o'clock in the morning just to afford them an opportunity to be in front of the long queues", and "patients with appointments for later times during the day still arrive substantially earlier nonetheless" so they will be served first-come-first-served. At Mulago's General OPD assessment centre, waiting time rose ~3–4 minutes per additional patient in the queue and was worse for those reporting after 11:00am — which makes early arrival the individually correct strategy.
  EVIDENCE: (a) Peer-reviewed scoping review, PLOS ONE 2024, mapping causes of long PHC waits in South Africa; (b) peer-reviewed qualitative study of nurse managers in Gauteng (PMC 2023); (c) Makerere dissertation/study on Mulago assessment-centre waiting time (~5 hours mean; queue-length and arrival-time effects). The Mulago figure is from a university-repository record and a ResearchGate copy, which is weaker sourcing than the peer-reviewed items.
  URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0299253
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes. South Africa is the best-documented SSA public system and its queue dynamics are directly analogous; the Mulago waiting-time work confirms the same incentive gradient locally. This is the strongest behavioural argument that 'join today's queue' is the culturally native act and 'book a slot' is the foreign one.

- CLAIM: Asking the patient WHEN they want to be seen does not remove a clinical judgement, it relabels one — and lay people are only ~50/50 at it. Systematic review of 19 studies: self-triage accuracy of laypeople was 47.3–62.4%; symptom-assessment apps 11.5–90.0%; LLMs 57.8–76.0%. A five-year follow-up of 22 symptom checkers found median triage accuracy of 55.8% (2020) vs 59.1% (2015) — no improvement — and that the 2020 apps "missed more than 40% of emergencies", with few apps outperforming laypersons.
  EVIDENCE: Peer-reviewed research: (a) npj Digital Medicine 2025 systematic review; (b) JMIR 2022, Schmieding et al., 5-year follow-up vignette study.
  URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01566-6
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly, and flag the WEIRD problem loudly. These cohorts are German, UK and US, generally literate, generally with primary-care alternatives. The _direction_ transfers — lay urgency judgement is unreliable — and plausibly worsens where health literacy is lower and where 'go to hospital today' is already the default heuristic. But no figure here should be quoted as a Ugandan number, because none of these studies enrolled anyone from a low-income country.

- CLAIM: The canonical Western justification for collapsing to one entry that asks a single question — GOV.UK's "one thing per page" — has NO published quantitative evidence behind it, by its own author's admission. The post asserts that "low-confidence users find them easier to use", that pages "work well on mobile devices" and handle "errors, branches, loops and saving progress" better. When a commenter asked for shareable quantitative data, Tim Paul (the GDS designer who wrote it) replied: "I wish we had some easy-to-share quant data on this as well, but I'm not aware of any." GOV.UK's own form-structure guidance says to split by default and to "only merge pages when you have clear evidence from user research that this would improve the user experience."
  EVIDENCE: Government design guidance + the author's own comment thread (opinion/practice-grade, explicitly not research). This is a negative finding about evidence quality, sourced from the primary artefact.
  URL: https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2015/07/03/one-thing-per-page/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Not as evidence — it never was evidence anywhere. It is UK civil-service craft convention derived from UK assisted-digital research. It should not be used to overturn a ratified ADR, and it also does not argue for one door: 'one thing per page' would put the book/queue choice on its own page, which is what two doors already is.

- CLAIM: Bandwidth and device constraints are real but do NOT discriminate between the two options — this is a red herring for this specific decision. Uganda's installed base is still feature-phone-dominated (roughly 32M feature phones vs ~20.5M smartphones on ~64.6M registered subscriptions as of mid-2026), UCC reports that many smartphone owners use devices mainly for calls, messaging and entertainment rather than digital services, and 1GB of data historically cost Ugandans around 8% of average monthly income against the UN Broadband Commission's "1 for 2" affordability target of 2%. But a screen with two labelled buttons and a screen with one question plus three answers are the same single round trip. What actually costs bandwidth is the scheduling engine behind door (A) — slot fetching, clinician lists, re-validation on submit.
  EVIDENCE: (b) vendor/regulator-reported statistics via national press (Daily Monitor, PC Tech Magazine reporting UCC sector reports) — these are secondary reports of UCC figures, not the UCC report itself, so treat the exact numbers as approximate; (a) A4AI/ITU affordability data as reported by Connecting Africa (2020 data: Africa average 1GB = 5.7% of monthly income; only 14 African countries meet the 2% target); (c) my own inference on the round-trip equivalence.
  URL: https://www.monitor.co.ug/uganda/business/prosper/30-years-later-feature-phones-still-outstrip-smartphones--5065896
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — it is Ugandan data. The actionable consequence is not about door count: it is that whichever door leads to slot-picking must not fetch a calendar of clinicians and slots over a metered 3G link, and that the queue path must be completable in one POST that survives a dropped connection (the offline-first pattern CommCare has used at scale in SSA, though that is vendor self-description, not an evaluation: https://commcare.dimagi.com/blog/collect-data-offline/).

- CLAIM: Any UI that renders a specific clock time is a mistranslation hazard in Uganda. East African languages including Luganda use a dawn-anchored clock with a 6-hour offset from the Western system: the Luganda "ssawa bbiri" literally means 'two hours' and denotes 8:00am, cognate with Swahili "saa mbili"; the convention is to "use the time system applicable to the language one happens to be speaking." A slot rendered as "08:00" or "2:00" can be read six hours away from what the scheduler meant.
  EVIDENCE: Reference/encyclopaedic source (Wikipedia, Date and time notation in Africa) corroborated by language-teaching sources. This is documented linguistic fact, not a usability study — I found NO HCI study measuring appointment-time misreading caused by this offset.
  URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_and_time_notation_in_Africa
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — it is specifically about Luganda and the East African region. Design consequence: prefer a queue POSITION ("you are 12th") or a day-part anchor ("morning, before lunch") over a bare clock time; if a clock time must be shown, anchor it ('8:00 in the morning'), never a bare 24-hour string. SARAL G2 supports positions/numbers as the comprehensible primitive.

BEARING ON ONE-DOOR-VS-TWO: From this angle the evidence does NOT support collapsing to one door, and I would recommend against it — but it also does not endorse the two doors as currently framed. Three points. (1) The HCI4D evidence everyone would cite for 'fewer doors' is actually about DEPTH, not width: Medhi et al. measured a 4-level nested tree against a flat list and recommended 'straight lists whenever possible, or very shallow hierarchies'. Two labelled buttons on one screen IS a flat list of length two — the shallowest structure there is. A single entry that asks 'today / soon / a specific date' is also a list of length three, and the third answer opens a calendar, which is a hierarchy (month, then week, then day, then time) and drags in date entry, on which the LMIC literature is completely silent and the dawn-offset Luganda clock makes actively hazardous. Collapsing to one door trades a one-level branch for a deeper one. That is the wrong direction. (2) Collapsing does not remove the clinical judgement, it hides it. 'Book or queue' and 'when do you want to be seen' are the same self-triage question in different clothes, and lay self-triage runs at 47-62% accuracy. Making it implicit means MedSync can no longer show a safety-net affordance against the wrong answer — which matters because this is a clinical product where decision support must never be autonomous. Two explicit doors are auditable and can each carry a 'if you are very unwell, come now' line; a wrapped 'when?' question cannot. (3) The real defect in the status quo is SYMMETRY, not count. The African public-hospital evidence is brutal about booking: 8.18% on-time arrival in the Nigerian audit, 20-40% DNA in South Africa, 74.9% of Ugandan HIV patients missing at least one visit, and patients queueing from 4am precisely to defeat scheduled times. Two visually equal doors give half the screen to the mechanism the hospital cannot honour. The recommendation that follows is: KEEP two doors (ADR 0034 holds), but make them asymmetric — 'Join today's queue' as the large primary action with the live queue position on it, 'Book a future visit' demoted to a secondary line beneath, ideally scoped to the follow-up/review cases where booking actually works. Additionally, from this angle specifically: label both doors in words (never icon-only — action icons are documented as misread), show queue POSITION rather than a clock time, anchor any clock time to a day-part, and make the queue-join one POST that survives a dropped connection. Finally, note honestly that the strongest Western argument for the one-door pattern, GOV.UK's 'one thing per page', is un-evidenced by its own author's admission and would in any case put the book/queue choice on its own page — i.e. it argues for what MedSync already has.
GAPS: NO EVIDENCE FOUND, anywhere, comparing a single 'when do you want to be seen' entry point against separate book/queue entry points in a health booking product. No A/B test, no usability study, no peer-reviewed comparison. Both designs are unevidenced; this decision cannot be settled by direct evidence and must be settled by transferring the depth/behaviour findings above.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND on date-picker or calendar-widget usability with low-literacy or low-education users in any LMIC. The HCI4D literature covers lists, menus, icons, audio and numerals extensively but is silent on date entry. Given that 'pick a specific date' is the hard half of door (A), this is the single most consequential hole in the literature for this decision.; NO PUBLISHED EVALUATION FOUND of digital queue-joining (remote ticket / SMS queue position) in an African public hospital. Searching returns only vendor marketing (Qmatic, Q-SYS, Qminder, QueueHub) describing their own products. The 'join today's queue' door is therefore as unevidenced as the booking door — it is justified by patient behaviour research, not by any study of the mechanism.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND on 12-hour vs 24-hour time format comprehension for East African populations. The dawn-offset clock is well documented linguistically; its effect on missed appointments has, as far as I can find, never been measured.; NO EVIDENCE FOUND on icon-only vs labelled navigation as a controlled comparison for these populations. The HCI4D corpus gives a strong indirect answer (action icons are misread; minimal text augmented by other modalities beats text-free) but I found no head-to-head study of labelled vs unlabelled navigation items.; NO USABILITY RESEARCH FOUND with Ugandan patients on any hospital booking or intake interface. Everything Uganda-specific I found is health-services research (waiting times, missed visits, satisfaction) or telecoms statistics, not interface research. The nearest HCI4D work is Indian, Bangladeshi, Pakistani and South African.; COULD NOT ACCESS the Johannesburg 'Booked Appointments System' feasibility study full text (medRxiv 403 on both the PDF and landing page), nor the ACM full texts for SARAL and Medhi CHI 2013. I worked from author-hosted PDFs and institutional summaries instead; the SARAL and INTERACT numbers are verbatim from author PDFs, the CHI 2013 figures are one step removed.

## The mechanics of the booking flow after the entry decision: slot selection, clinician choice, step count, confirmation/reminders, reschedule vs cancel, and approval-required vs instant booking.

- CLAIM: Booking horizon is itself a no-show generator: no-show rates rise steeply and monotonically with lead time, and the effect is far worse in the high-baseline-no-show clinic. Showing patients a long calendar is therefore not a neutral UI choice — it manufactures DNAs.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (retrospective analysis, 46,655 appointments over 12 months, University of Virginia Eye Clinic; Clinical Ophthalmology 2015). At 0–2 weeks lead time the no-show rate was 9.1% (resident clinic) and 2.4% (faculty clinic); at 6 months lead time it rose to 38.3% and 6.9% respectively. Predictive model: the resident-clinic no-show rate falls 1.7 percentage points for every 10% of appointments moved into a 0–2 week lead time, and would drop by ~60% if all appointments were booked within 2 weeks. Authors explicitly recommend an 'advanced or open access' scheduling system for clinic populations with high and variable no-show rates.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4370946/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, with the caveat that it is a US academic eye clinic. The mechanism (time decay of intent, life circumstances changing, transport and money not being available on the day) is not culture-specific, and the resident-clinic arm — high baseline no-show, trainee-staffed, teaching hospital — is the closer analogue to a Mulago OPD than the faculty arm. If anything the gradient should be steeper in Kampala, where transport cost and informal work make a date three months out less plannable, not more.

- CLAIM: Never ask a patient for a date. Government service-design guidance is explicit that a typed date input is only for dates the user already knows, and that a calendar picker earns its place mainly in the 'available dates only' case — which is exactly the sparse-availability case MedSync is in.
  EVIDENCE: VENDOR/GOVERNMENT SERVICE MANUAL (primary, and research-backed in general though not for this specific question). GOV.UK Design System, date input: 'Use the date input component when you're asking users for a date they'll already know, or can look up without using a calendar… Do not use the date input component if users are unlikely to know the exact date of the event you're asking about.' MOJ Design System, date picker: use a picker 'for a relative date or one they need to look up, for example last Thursday or next Wednesday; to enter today's date more quickly; for available dates only, such as for prison visits' — and avoid it 'when only a rough date is needed'. MOJ also warns date pickers 'are fully navigable using a keyboard, but can be slow for keyboard-only and screen reader users'.
  URL: https://design-patterns.service.justice.gov.uk/components/date-picker
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes. This is guidance about the user's state of knowledge, not about UK culture. A walk-in patient has a rough need ('today', 'this week', 'after payday') and no date in mind, which is precisely the case both manuals tell you not to put a date field in front of. The accessibility point strengthens in a mixed-literacy, shared-device, small-screen context: a list of two or three concrete offered times is readable aloud to someone else; a month grid is not.

- CLAIM: When a public system offers patients choice at the point of booking, most of them do not use it and a large share never touch the booking UI at all — someone books on their behalf. Designing the flow around the patient choosing a named clinician is designing for a minority.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (structured exit survey of 104 patients at a London hospital, May–Aug 2006; BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making 2008). Of Choose & Book patients: 53% were booked inside the GP surgery (and of those, 83% were booked by the GP, not the patient), 36% used the phone booking line, and only 11% booked online themselves. 66% reported not being given a choice of appointment date; 66% reported no choice of time; 32% reported no choice of hospital; exactly one patient (2%) received the full intended set of choices. The authors attribute the failure partly to 'patients often prefer delegating decisions to healthcare professionals' alongside GP framing and low awareness.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2529277/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly, and the direction of the bias favours the same conclusion more strongly. The study is a UK public system in 2006 with far better connectivity and literacy than a Mulago OPD, and even there 89% did not self-serve online. In Kampala, proxy booking (a relative, a clerk, a nurse with the ward tablet) will be more common, not less. What does not transfer is the 2006 technology baseline — online booking has since become ordinary — so treat the 11% as a floor, not a forecast.

- CLAIM: The 'too many choices paralyses people' argument is not a safe citation. The canonical jam-study effect has not held up in meta-analysis; mean effect is approximately zero across the literature, with effects appearing only under specific moderators.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments, N = 5,036; Journal of Consumer Research 2010, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd) found a mean effect size of virtually zero with large between-study variance; a failed direct replication of Iyengar & Lepper's jam study is part of that record. A later conceptual review and meta-analysis (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, Journal of Consumer Psychology 2015) reframes it as moderator-dependent (choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, decision goal) rather than a main effect of option count.
  URL: https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Not directly — the underlying experiments are WEIRD consumer-lab studies (jams, chocolates, pens) with no healthcare or LMIC replication that I could find. The useful transfer is negative: do NOT justify 'assign the clinician' by citing choice overload. Justify it from the Choose & Book delegation finding and from Mulago's actual rota reality instead. Note also that the surviving moderator most relevant here — preference uncertainty — is high for a patient who has no basis to prefer one internal-medicine registrar over another, which is an argument for a sensible default rather than for hiding the option.

- CLAIM: Patients do value choosing their clinician, and will trade real waiting days for it — but the trade flips by clinical context. For acute, worrying problems speed wins; for routine and continuity-of-care visits the named clinician wins by a wide margin.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (stated-preference discrete choice experiments). Rubin et al., 'Preferences for access to the GP: a discrete choice experiment', BJGP 2006: speed of access was of limited importance and for many patients was outweighed by choice of GP or convenience of appointment, though wait time remained a significant driver. A later DCE reports patients willing to wait ~5 days longer to see a doctor of their choice for an acute, low-worry symptom; for a routine check-up, willingness to wait an extra 3.5 days to see a GP rather than a nurse, 4.2 days for relational continuity and 7.8 days for informational continuity; for a minor familiar symptom the same trade-offs shrink to ~1 day, 0.9 days and 1.6 days.
  URL: https://bjgp.org/content/56/531/743
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. These are UK GP-registration systems where patients have a personal doctor to be continuous with — a relationship that largely does not exist at a Ugandan national referral hospital running cover-and-rotate rotas, so relational continuity is often not on offer to trade for. Informational continuity (the next clinician has your record) is the part MedSync can actually deliver, and in the DCE it was valued more highly than relational continuity for routine visits (7.8 vs 4.2 days). Design implication that does transfer: sell 'your record follows you', not 'your doctor'.

- CLAIM: One-way SMS reminders are the single best-evidenced intervention available here, and the evidence base is African rather than borrowed. Two-way conversational SMS reminders show no attendance benefit — build the cheap one, not the clever one.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (two systematic reviews with meta-analysis, PLOS One). One-way SMS vs no SMS on appointment attendance: OR 2.03 (95% CI 1.40–2.95), 12 trials, 6,448 participants, all sub-Saharan African settings, I² = 85%; subgroup effects ranged from childhood immunisation OR 3.69 (1.67–8.13) down to HIV appointment attendance OR 1.48 (0.73–3.00, not significant). Critically, low-risk-of-bias trials showed a much weaker effect (OR 1.36) than high-risk trials (OR 2.62), and the authors state the true effect 'may be lower'. The companion review of two-way text messaging found no improvement in appointment attendance: RR 1.03 (95% CI 0.95–1.12), 5 trials, 4,374 participants — while medicine adherence did improve, RR 1.14 (1.07–1.21).
  URL: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0217485
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes — this is the most directly transferable evidence in this report. Every trial in both reviews was set in sub-Saharan Africa (one Egyptian exception), in low-bandwidth, feature-phone-inclusive conditions. Two operational riders: the honest effect size is nearer OR 1.4 than 2.0 once bias is accounted for, and the delivery channel must be plain SMS, not an in-app push that assumes data.

- CLAIM: A reminder without a self-service way to cancel or move the appointment makes things worse, not better — and freed capacity is wasted unless release and rebooking are automatic. This is the strongest available argument for wiring MedSync's existing reschedule Edge Function to a UI, and for putting reschedule ahead of cancel.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (four-phase PDSA quality improvement study, 1,500 outpatient slots, general surgery clinics, Mid and South Essex NHS Foundation Trust, Sept 2023–Mar 2025; BMJ Open Quality 2026). Phone reminders (Phase 3) cut DNA from 12.2% baseline to 9.4% and short-notice cancellations from 5.0% to 0.6%. Text reminders with no embedded cancellation route (Phase 4) cut DNA further to 8.1% but pushed short-notice cancellations UP to 7.2%, because patients 'recognise they could not attend' but were 'offered no embedded cancellation mechanism'. Of the 35 patients who asked to cancel and rebook in Phase 3, only 20 freed slots were reused — 15 were lost 'due to lack of appointment booking staff'. 30% of contactable DNA patients had tried to reach the hospital to cancel and failed, citing hold times and 'not enough credit (in the phone) to call to cancel'; ~20% of DNA patients had stale contact details. Authors' conclusion: 'sustainable improvement in OPA utilisation is not primarily a reminder-design problem but a system-design problem requiring reliable contact data, accessible cancellation routes, bidirectional communication channels, and real-time administrative capacity.'
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13422818/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes, and more acutely. The airtime-credit barrier ('not enough credit to call to cancel') appears in an NHS study and is a daily reality in Uganda; the stale-contact-details problem is worse where numbers churn between SIMs. The staff bottleneck on re-booking freed slots transfers directly to a scarce-clerk referral hospital and argues for automatic slot release on cancel/reschedule with no human in the loop. Caveat: this is a single-consultant QI project, not a randomised trial, so the phase-to-phase numbers are suggestive rather than causal.

- CLAIM: When a booking system has no slot to offer, it does not gracefully fail — it silently converts the patient into a worklist item that the provider must chase. The NHS has an entire named operational category for this.
  EVIDENCE: VENDOR/GOVERNMENT OPERATIONAL DOCUMENTATION plus press coverage. In the NHS e-Referral Service, where a referral is made and no appointment slot is available, the referral is 'deferred to the provider', who must contact the patient directly; each deferral lands on the provider's Appointment Slot Issue (ASI) worklist, and an unbooked referral is removed from the system after 180 days. Patient-safety campaigners have called for the growing scale of ASIs to be urgently investigated. (I could not retrieve the NHS Digital ASI guidance page itself — HTTP 403 — so the scale figures below are second-hand.)
  URL: https://digital.nhs.uk/services/e-referral-service/document-library/managing-and-minimising-appointment-slot-issues
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The NHS referral-management machinery does not exist at Mulago, but the failure mode is the general one for any slot-picking UI laid over sparse capacity, which is exactly Mulago's position. The transferable lesson is structural: a 'pick a slot' flow must have a defined, patient-visible destination when there are no slots, or it becomes an invisible queue with worse properties than a visible one.

- CLAIM: There is no published evidence on whether an approval-required booking ('request sent, awaiting clinician confirmation') harms trust relative to an instant confirmation. The numbers circulating on this are scheduling-SaaS marketing with no stated method.
  EVIDENCE: GAP, plus two weak datapoints. VENDOR (own product, 2017): Airbnb states 'more than 40 percent of listings on Airbnb are instantly bookable', '70 percent of new listings… have hosts using Instant Book from the very beginning' and '60 percent of bookings by guests are now secured with Instant Book' — a revealed-preference signal that instant beats request at scale, but from a marketplace with no clinical stakes and no comparative data given. VENDOR (own product): Zocdoc describes booking as instant — availability 'pulled directly from the practice's scheduling system… real openings you can book right now' and 'written directly into the provider's schedule' — while its provider help centre simultaneously shows a 'New booking' notification that practices must confirm from an Inbox, i.e. the patient sees a confirmed booking while the provider confirmation happens invisibly behind it. Everything else I found (e.g. 'instant confirmation increases show rates by 25%', '4.2% vs 41.3% abandonment for automated vs manual confirmation') traces to scheduling-software blogs with no methodology, sample or source.
  URL: https://news.airbnb.com/instant-book-updates
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: No — neither datapoint is healthcare in a low-resource public setting, and the marketing figures should not be cited at all. The design lesson that does survive is Zocdoc's structure rather than its numbers: present the patient with an unambiguous outcome and keep the clinician's confirmation step behind the scenes. For MedSync that maps onto a hard clinical-safety constraint — a queue position is not a promise of being seen, and must never be worded as if it were.

- CLAIM: No credible step-count or abandonment data exists for healthcare booking funnels. The only rigorous funnel research is e-commerce checkout, and the healthcare figures in circulation are SEO content.
  EVIDENCE: VENDOR RESEARCH, e-commerce (Baymard Institute, ~10 years of large-scale checkout testing): 17% of US online shoppers have abandoned an order due to a 'too long / complicated checkout process'; the average US checkout shows 23.48 form elements (14.88 fields) by default versus an ideal of 12–14 elements (7–8 fields); Baymard claims a large site can gain a 35.26% conversion increase through better checkout design. By contrast, the healthcare-specific claims returned by search ('most healthcare websites require 6–8 steps to book', 'top practices achieve 21.1% conversion') come exclusively from scheduling-vendor and agency marketing blogs with no method or sample disclosed. INFERENCE: the transferable, defensible statement is about field count and page weight, not about a magic number of steps.
  URL: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. Baymard's panel is US/EU e-commerce on good connections; a Ugandan patient on intermittent, metered data faces a harsher version of the same penalty, because each extra step is not just cognitive cost but a round trip that can fail. The direction transfers, the magnitudes do not. The practical rule that survives: minimise round trips and fields, and make every step survive a dropped connection — not 'get it under N screens'.

- CLAIM: The one Uganda-specific appointment-app study is encouraging but must not be used as evidence for Mulago — its sample has 100% smartphone ownership and 100% internet use, which is a private-hospital population, not a national referral OPD.
  EVIDENCE: RESEARCH (cross-sectional survey, five PRIVATE hospitals in Mbarara Municipality, southwestern Uganda; 97 patients/caregivers and 32 providers; published 2026). Willingness to use the MobiCare scheduling app: 100% of patients/caregivers, 96.9% of providers, 99% would recommend it. Reported infrastructure: 100% smartphone ownership, 100% internet use, 99% had previously used a mobile app — but only 8.2% were aware of patient-provider linking apps. Current appointment-making was informal and voice-first: 69% by phone call, 7% by WhatsApp. 75% of those with waiting experience were dissatisfied, with waits averaging 120 minutes. Separately, an exit survey at seven Mulago outpatient clinics found overall satisfaction suboptimal and significantly lower among patients waiting more than 2 hours.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13034015/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: This IS Uganda, and that is precisely the trap. The sample is self-selected private-hospital attendees in a university town; 100% smartphone-and-internet penetration is not the Mulago OPD population, so its 100% willingness figure is close to useless as a demand estimate for the beachhead. Two things do transfer: the incumbent behaviour is a phone call to a person (69%), so the flow competes with calling, not with a web form; and the pain being solved is the 2-hour wait, which is a queue-visibility problem more than a booking problem.

BEARING ON ONE-DOOR-VS-TWO: It bears on it, and it leans toward one door — but for mechanical rather than aesthetic reasons, and with one hard condition. Three pieces of evidence converge. (1) Lead time is toxic: no-shows run 9.1% at 0–2 weeks and 38.3% at 6 months in the high-no-show clinic arm (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4370946/), and the authors recommend open-access scheduling outright. At Mulago the honest bookable horizon is therefore days, not months, which means door A and door B are separated by hours of real difference, not by a category difference — two doors advertise a distinction the underlying capacity cannot deliver. (2) Government service-design guidance says not to ask users for a date they do not have in mind, and reserves calendar pickers for the available-dates-only case (https://design-patterns.service.justice.gov.uk/components/date-picker). A single "when do you need to be seen — today / in the next few days / a specific date" question is exactly the sanctioned shape; door A's implied calendar is exactly the discouraged one. (3) Slot-picking flows fail ungracefully when capacity is sparse: the NHS had to invent the Appointment Slot Issue worklist for the case where a patient is referred and no slot exists (https://digital.nhs.uk/services/e-referral-service/document-library/managing-and-minimising-appointment-slot-issues). One entry that asks WHEN can answer "no slot — here is your place in today's queue" as a routing outcome; two doors force the patient to have already chosen the door that dead-ends. The hard condition, and it is a clinical-safety condition rather than a UX preference: collapsing the entry must NOT collapse the confirmation. A booked slot is a promise; a queue position is not. The only defensible pattern I found for hiding a mechanism behind a single entry is Zocdoc's — present one unambiguous outcome to the user and keep the provider-side confirmation invisible (https://www.zocdoc.com/blog/facts/real-time-availability-instant-booking/) — and there is NO published evidence that request-versus-instant framing affects trust, so this must be validated with users rather than cited. Two caveats against over-reading me. First, my angle is the mechanics after entry; it says the machinery behind door A is fragile here, which is an argument about what one door must route INTO, not proof that users find two doors confusing — I found no usability study comparing single versus split entry for booking-vs-walk-in. Second, ADR 0034's own framing ("intake is a status, not an entry type") is already consistent with one door; if the two doors exist only as presentation over a single status model, this is a UI change, not an architecture reversal. Separately and independent of the entry question, the evidence strongly supports three build decisions: one-way SMS reminders, not two-way conversational ones (OR 2.03 vs RR 1.03, all African trials — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0217485); wiring the dormant reschedule Edge Function to a UI reachable in one tap from the reminder, with reschedule placed above cancel and automatic slot release, because reminders without an embedded cancellation route pushed short-notice cancellations from 0.6% to 7.2% and 43% of freed slots went unused for want of a clerk (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13422818/); and defaulting the clinician assignment rather than making the patient pick, since only 11% of Choose & Book patients booked themselves at all and the authors found patients often prefer to delegate (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2529277/) — justified on that evidence, NOT on choice overload, which does not survive meta-analysis (https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/37/3/409/1827647).
GAPS: No usability research found comparing calendar-grid slot pickers against list-of-times or 'next available' presentations, in healthcare or anywhere else. Every source making this comparison was a design blog or a component-library marketing page with no research behind it.; No evidence found on how many days of availability to show at once. The only figures found ('show one month at a time on mobile') trace to scheduling-vendor blogs with no study.; No evidence found on how to present a clinician with sparse availability. The NHS ASI material documents that the failure exists and what the operational fallback is, but says nothing about the patient-facing UI, and I could not retrieve the primary NHS Digital guidance page (HTTP 403) for scale figures on how often it happens.; No peer-reviewed or otherwise methodologically credible evidence found on approval-required ('request pending clinician confirmation') versus instant confirmation and its effect on user trust, perceived reliability, or attendance — in healthcare or adjacent domains. All specific numbers found (25% show-rate uplift, 4.2% vs 41.3% abandonment, '78% expect confirmation within 60 seconds') come from scheduling-SaaS marketing content with no stated sample, method, or date. This is a genuine hole and should be treated as a user-research question for MedSync, not a settled pattern.; No published conversion or abandonment data found for healthcare booking funnels broken down by step count. The healthcare figures in circulation are agency and vendor marketing; the only rigorous funnel research (Baymard) is US/EU e-commerce checkout on good connections and transfers only in direction, not magnitude.; No trial or A/B evidence found on the relative prominence of reschedule versus cancel affordances. The BMJ Open Quality study establishes that an accessible cancellation route matters a great deal, but nothing found tests reschedule-above-cancel versus cancel-above-reschedule, or measures how many cancellations convert to rebookings when reschedule is made the primary action.; No evidence found from a Ugandan PUBLIC referral hospital on digital booking uptake, slot-picking behaviour, or proxy booking. The single Uganda appointment-app study is five private hospitals in Mbarara with 100% smartphone ownership and 100% internet use, which cannot stand in for a Mulago OPD population. This is the most important gap for the decision and is only closable by fieldwork at the beachhead.; No evidence found on shared-device or proxy booking (a relative or clerk booking on a patient's behalf) in any booking-UX literature, despite Choose & Book showing 83% of in-surgery bookings were made by the GP rather than the patient. Nothing found on how confirmations, reminders, or reschedule links should be addressed when the phone number on file belongs to someone other than the patient.; No usability study found comparing a single 'when do you need care?' entry against separate book-versus-walk-in entry points. The closest adjacent work is ED pre-triage self-registration (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11193078/), which compares kiosk versus smartphone entry to the same flow, not one door versus two.

## Failure modes and anti-patterns — what goes wrong in medical booking systems, and what that implies for one door vs two

- CLAIM: Patient-declared urgency is close to statistically worthless as a clinical signal: in 888 matched patient/clinician pairs in Australian after-hours primary care, agreement on urgency was kappa 0.166 ('slight') and on 'safe to wait' kappa 0.209 ('fair'). 31.5% of patients who called their problem urgent were assessed non-urgent; ~17% who called it non-urgent were assessed urgent.
  EVIDENCE: Research — cross-sectional matched surveys, 888 matched records across 50 general practices, 3 deputising services, 3 nurse-led walk-in centres and 2 EDs (BMC Health Services Research, 2023). The bidirectional disagreement is the load-bearing number: the dangerous direction is the ~17% who self-declare NON-urgent while being clinically urgent.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152437/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The effect (patients cannot rank their own urgency) is about human symptom interpretation, not health-system structure, so it plausibly transfers. The magnitudes do not: the Australian cohort had an appointment-literate population with a familiar GP, and the paper notes agreement was substantially HIGHER for patients seeing a clinician they knew. At Mulago, with walk-in culture, no continuity and mixed literacy, agreement should be expected to be worse, not better.

- CLAIM: Independently replicated in an ED setting: across 8 Queensland public EDs (n=417), only 30.9% of patients' self-perceived priority matched their assigned triage category (kappa 0.07, no statistically significant agreement). 48.5% expected a HIGHER priority than assigned; 20.6% expected a LOWER one — i.e. one in five patients under-triaged themselves.
  EVIDENCE: Research — cross-sectional survey with medical-record linkage (Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine, 2016).
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5070359/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Yes for the direction and the existence of a substantial self-under-triage group. No for the base rates — Queensland EDs see a different case mix, and the 31% response rate biases toward patients well enough to consent.

- CLAIM: The patients most likely to mis-rate urgency are the ones with low health literacy — the exact group MedSync's 'mixed literacy' constraint names.
  EVIDENCE: Research — multicentre German survey (Frontiers in Public Health, 2025) finding low health literacy independently associated with judging low-acuity presentations as highly urgent, while adequate-health-literacy patients aligned more closely with nurse triage categories.
  URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1656755/full
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly, and the transfer argument cuts BOTH ways. The mechanism (health literacy moderates self-assessment accuracy) is general. But this study measured OVER-rating by low-literacy patients in a system where everyone can reach an ED free at the point of use. In a Ugandan context where a hospital trip costs money and a day's income, the literature on care-seeking suggests the low-literacy failure is more likely to be delayed presentation (under-rating), not over-rating. I found no Ugandan study measuring this directly — see gaps.

- CLAIM: NHS England's own operational guidance tells practices NOT to accept urgent clinical requests through the online funnel at all: 'urgent clinical requests should be made in person or via telephone, to avoid urgent clinical requests being incorrectly submitted online.' This is an explicit, official endorsement of a SEPARATE door for 'I need care now' rather than a single funnel with an urgency field.
  EVIDENCE: Vendor/government guidance — NHS England, 'Online consultations – frequently asked questions and support resources'. Note the same page also says 111 'would not constitute an appropriate alternative service during core hours', i.e. the separate door must land somewhere real, not be a deflection.
  URL: https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/online-consultations-frequently-asked-questions/
  TRANSFERS TO UGANDA: Partly. The safety logic (an asynchronous funnel is the wrong container for a time-critical need) transfers directly and is arguably stronger in Uganda, where intermittent bandwidth means an online submission may not reach anyone for hours. What does NOT transfer is the assumed fallback: NHS England can assume a phone line answered by a practice in core hours. MedSync's equivalent fallback is physical presence at the hospital, which is what the queue door already models.

- CLAIM: A 'when do you need care?' question that routes clinically is self-triage, and self-triage software has a documented and unflattering safety ceiling: online symptom checkers asked only 36.9% of relevant red flags versus clinicians' 71.8%, and correctly triaged 76.9% of emergency vignettes. Ada's urgency advice exactly matched the gold standard in 63% of vignettes.
  EVIDENCE: Research — red-flag coverage study (BMC Health Services Research, 2025) and an independently-generated vignette study of Ada (preprint, medRxiv 2020). Both are vignette studies, not live-patient outcomes, which is the standard limitation of this literature.
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12486864/

BEARING ON ONE-DOOR-VS-TWO: The failure-mode evidence does not settle one-door-vs-two, because it says the real hazards are almost entirely orthogonal to that choice — and it says so clearly enough to be a recommendation in its own right. Three things follow.

(1) The documented anti-pattern is asking patients to choose between things they cannot distinguish (the urgent-care sense-making study: convenience and waiting time, not clinical need, drive the choice — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6624886/). MedSync's two doors are not that. 'Book a future visit' vs 'Join today's queue' is already a WHEN question rendered as two buttons; 'today' vs 'a specific date' is a temporal fact a patient can answer reliably, unlike 'is my problem urgent?' or 'which service is right for me?'. Collapsing them into one screen that asks 'today / soon / a specific date' would produce the same three branches from the same reliable question. On this angle's evidence the collapse is cosmetic, and I would not spend the migration on it. If it is done, do it because the two-button screen is one extra tap on a slow connection, not because two doors is a documented hazard — because it is not, and I found no evidence that it is.

(2) The change that IS supported by evidence is removing `urgent_review` from the patient-selectable values of `appointments.reason` (db/schema/scheduling.ts:191 and :368). Two independent studies put patient/clinician urgency agreement at kappa 0.166 and kappa 0.07, with ~17–21% of patients rating themselves LESS urgent than clinicians did (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10152437/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5070359/). A patient-picked 'urgent_review' is therefore not a triage signal, but it looks like one sitting next to the system-driven `system_urgent` origin in the same table — two values with near-identical names and completely different evidential weight, which is a classifier waiting to be misread by a worklist query or a future dashboard. Note the dangerous direction is under-declaration: the patient who picks 'routine' and books for next month while having a red flag is invisible to BOTH doors and to a one-door 'when' question alike. No entry-point design fixes that. Only an early red-flag question does — and NHS England's guidance is to keep urgent needs out of the asynchronous funnel entirely ('urgent clinical requests should be made in person or via telephone' — https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/online-consultations-frequently-asked-questions/), which is an argument for a distinct, prominently-placed 'come now / call' route rather than a smarter router.

(3) If the collapse to one door IS made, the evidence attaches a condition: show the routing decision, never hide it. Hidden adjudication is the documented failure of NHS total triage — patients feel 'fobbed off', trust erodes, and the burden falls on those least able to frame their need in the system's vocabulary (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953624003290), which is precisely MedSync's mixed-literacy population. And HSSIB's August 2026 finding of 30 near misses and harms from an inadequately designed and monitored digital routing layer (https://www.pulsetoday.co.uk/news/clinical-areas/cardiovascular/advice-and-guidance-contributed-to-patient-harm-nhs-safety-investigation-finds) means the router would itself become a clinical mechanism requiring audit and monitoring. Two visible doors have no router to audit. That is a real, if unglamorous, safety advantage of the status quo.

Verdict from this angle: keep two doors, or collapse them only as a layout change that preserves the visible 'today vs later' distinction and the visible destination. Spend the effort instead on (a) deleting patient-selectable `urgent_review`, (b) an active 'do you have any of these symptoms right now?' red-flag exit placed BEFORE the branch rather than a passive banner beside it — noting honestly that I found no evidence validating disclaimer placement, so this is reasoned design, not proven practice — and (c) writing ADR 0034 §6's honesty principle (position-in-line only, no invented wait estimate) into a standing rule that also forbids slot-scarcity cues in the booking calendar, which are a named harmful practice and now strict-liability banned in the UK (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf).
GAPS: No controlled evidence — no RCT, no published usability study, no A/B result — comparing a two-door entry (choose mechanism) against a single 'when do you need care?' entry in any healthcare booking product. I searched for this directly and found nothing. Every claim either side of this decision is an extrapolation from adjacent literature. Anyone asserting a measured conversion or safety difference between the two designs is inventing it.; No empirical evidence on whether emergency disclaimers ('if this is an emergency, call 999/911') in a digital health flow actually change behaviour, or on where they should be placed. I searched specifically for warning-effectiveness studies in telemedicine/booking contexts and found none. What I could verify: the 111 start page branches by CATEGORY OF NEED (symptoms/injury, dental, mental health, sexual health, medicines), not by mechanism — which is itself relevant. But the nhs.uk 'when to use 111' page places its 'call 999 now' line in a section BELOW the 111 online entry point, not above it, which contradicts the design-blog assumption that the emergency exit always leads. I could not fetch the in-flow triage screens to confirm whether red-flag questions come first.; No evidence found on how Uganda's National Drug Authority (or any East African regulator) treats software-as-a-medical-device or patient-facing triage software.
